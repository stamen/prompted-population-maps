How we got here

Texas, prompt by prompt

The style was developed on Texas through a conversation with an AI coding agent. Each step shows the image, what was asked for, and what changed in the code.

First test render

“Give me a population density map of Texas… extruded… dramatic raytraced shadows… red-white-blue. Use real data, and real shadows.”

An 800 px, 16-sample check of the pipeline: 317,903 Kontur hexagons became 2.2 million faces and path-traced in 8 seconds. Peaks were capped at 42 km and the base was light grey, so it read as flat and the white middle class blended into the base.

First full render

“(first delivered map)”

Peaks raised to 110 km and the sun lowered to 7.5° from the west-northwest so shadows run east across the state. Charcoal base, grey ground, gradient legend, title “Where 30 million people live”.

Brighter, taller, warmer, labelled

“Trim the scale so the zero point is a brighter red and the top is a brighter blue… boost the vertical scale… lighten and warm up the background… add annotation for the major city spikes.”

Darkest ends of the ramp trimmed, peaks to 180 km, sand ground, twelve cities with leader lines and peak densities. Labels anchor on the visible skyline (25th-tallest nearby cell) because the densest cell is an invisible needle.

Uninhabited land in red

“The gray near-zero population background of the map should be red too.”

Kontur only lists inhabited hexagons, so empty land showed the base slab. The slab took the exact colour of the lowest class.

Names only, five swatches

“Remove the density numbers… remove the leader lines… boost the vertical scale even more… simplify the legend into just 5 swatches.”

Peaks to 280 km (2.5× the first render), plain names on each skyline, five swatches at 1, 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000+ people per km².

Plan oblique view

“Stretch the image vertically so the image is closer to the proportions of Texas as viewed top down, but keep the perspective view.”

Blender renders with wide pixels (aspect 1/cos 50°): the ground keeps its top-down shape while heights still rise at the 50° view. Lighting is untouched and label positions come from Blender’s own camera projection.

Simpler title and a colour grade

“Change the title to a simpler ‘Texas Population Density’… contrast, brightness, or other levels adjustments… bright and clean-looking.”

Levels put the sand near white, a gentle S-curve kept shadows strong, greys were neutralised and reds pulled back from neon. Legend swatches are rendered as flat tiles under the map’s lighting so they match after the grade.

Readable labels and a stepped legend

“The place labels need adjustment so they’re a bit easier to read. Also the scale needs an extra callout… or make each swatch a different height.”

Bold names on cream pills with a faint outline and shadow. The swatches rise one step per 10×, with callouts: darkest blue is 10,000× denser than darkest red, and heights are linear (the tallest spike’s real value is printed).

2026 Senate results draped on population

“Source county level election results from the 2026 Texas US Senate race and drape those colors on the population map instead of coloring by density.”

254-county results (Wikipedia, spot-checked against Decision Desk HQ and the Dallas Morning News). Each hexagon takes its county’s two-party margin; uninhabited land is neutral grey.

County-coloured land

“In this case I would color the slab according the county election results, too.”

The land slab is built from the 254 Census county polygons, each in its result colour, so empty counties read correctly and spikes show where the votes are.

Precinct-level 2024 results

“Search the web for better resolution results, like at the precinct level… you can probably find it for the 2024 presidential results.”

2026 precinct data is not yet published statewide, so this uses the NYT’s 9,718 Texas precincts for 2024 (totals within 0.1% of certified). Cities split into blue cores and red suburbs; the Rio Grande Valley shows the 2024 shift.

Sun at 15°, centred labels

“Your city labels aren't correctly centered in their white background pill shapes… bring [the light] up higher above the horizon, try 15º.”

Names are centred on cap height. Shadows are half as long. The grade now measures the bare ground in each render, so the background stays the same cream at any sun angle.

Lessons

Things that will bite you

The densest cell is a needle

A city's densest hexagon is one pixel wide and towers over the visible skyline. Labels anchor on the 25th-tallest cell nearby instead, which is where the eye sees the city.

H3 cells are not always hexagons

Resolution-7 cells that straddle an icosahedron face edge carry up to 10 boundary vertices. prep.py keeps the six sharpest corners; pentagons are padded to six.

Lakes need two polygons

Clip the population with the full provincial boundary and build the land from the lake-cut shape. Clipping data with 1:10m lake shorelines dropped 780,000 Ontario lakeshore residents.

Legend colours must be rendered

Lighting and tone mapping change how each colour looks. The swatches are flat tiles rendered under the identical sun and sky, then put through the same grade as the map.

Dense countries look different

Germany, the UK and India are blue almost everywhere because rural density is in the hundreds per km². England's 99th-percentile cell is a quarter of its maximum, so its field is uniformly tall. That is the data.

Check totals, and check sources

prep.py prints the population it kept: Texas 29.95M, Germany 83.5M, India 1.43B, China 1.43B all match official figures. Election totals were checked against certified or independently reported numbers.

# AGENTS.md: working on prompted-population-maps

Instructions for any coding agent (Claude, Codex, Gemini, Cursor, aider, …) or person continuing this project.
Read this file, then `README.md`. Everything you need to reproduce or extend the maps is in this repo.

## What this repo makes

3D population maps: every inhabited H3 hexagon from Kontur Population is extruded to a height that is
**linear in people per km²**, then path-traced in Blender Cycles (via the `bpy` Python module) under a low sun,
so shadows are real ray-traced shadows. Colour is either population density (log scale) or an election
result (two-party margin, county or precinct), draped over the same population geometry.

## Setup

```bash
uv venv -p 3.13 renv            # or: python3.13 -m venv renv
uv pip install -p renv/bin/python -r requirements.txt
export PYTHON=renv/bin/python
pipeline/fetch_data.sh US IN FR GB CN HK MO DE CA   # Kontur files by ISO-2 code + boundaries (~2.5 GB)
pipeline/fetch_data.sh --elections                  # optional: Texas election data
```

No Blender app is needed; `bpy` is Blender as a Python module. Rendering is CPU-only by default.

## Commands

```bash
pipeline/make_map.sh germany preview   # 800 px, 16 samples, ~20-45 s  -> out/germany_preview.png
pipeline/make_map.sh germany final     # 3600 px, 64 samples, 6-15 min on 4 cores -> out/germany.png
python docs/build_site.py              # rebuild the GitHub Pages site from out/*.png and pipeline/*
```

`make_map.sh` runs, in order: `legend_render.py` (swatch colours under the current sun), `prep.py` (only if
`work/<region>.npz` is missing), `render.py`, `annotate.py`. Delete `work/<region>.npz` after changing anything
that `prep.py` reads (boundaries, clipping, resolution, election data).

## Repo map

| Path | Role |
|---|---|
| `pipeline/regions.py` | **All per-map configuration**, paths, sun angle (`SUN_EL`, `SUN_AZ`), election colour ramp |
| `pipeline/prep.py` | Clip Kontur cells to the boundary, aggregate to H3 res 7 for regions wider than 2,000 km, project (Lambert azimuthal equal-area), join election results, write `work/<region>.npz` |
| `pipeline/render.py` | Build the Blender scene (hex prisms, land slab, ground, sun, sky, plan-oblique camera), export label anchors, path-trace |
| `pipeline/annotate.py` | Colour grade, title, legend, callouts, credits, city labels with collision avoidance |
| `pipeline/legend_render.py` | Render flat swatch tiles under the map's exact lighting so legend colours match the map |
| `pipeline/fetch_data.sh`, `fetch_tx_senate_2026.py` | Data downloads |
| `docs/` | GitHub Pages site (`index.html` is generated by `docs/build_site.py` from `docs/template.html`) |
| `fonts/` | Inter (SIL OFL 1.1), used by `annotate.py` |

## Adding a region

1. Add an entry to `R` in `pipeline/regions.py`. Copy a similar one:
   - `kontur`: ISO-2 codes of the Kontur files (fetch them with `fetch_data.sh`).
   - `bnd`: `('ne0', [Natural Earth ADMIN names])` for countries, `('ne1', 'Province')` for provinces
     (prep.py filters `admin=='Canada'`; generalise that filter for other countries), `('census', 'ST')` for US states.
   - `national=True` only when the Kontur file covers exactly that territory (keeps coastal cells with a 3 km buffer).
   - optional `bbox` (drop overseas territories), `lakes=True` (cut Natural Earth lakes from the land slab).
   - `cities`: `(name, lon, lat)` in label priority order; colliding labels are nudged, then dropped.
2. `pipeline/make_map.sh <region> preview` and **look at the image**.
3. Check the population total `prep.py` prints against an official figure. Within ~2% is normal.
4. Render `final`, then `python docs/build_site.py` if it should appear on the site.

## Election maps

Add `election=dict(...)` to a region (see `texas_senate` and `texas_pres2024`):
- `level='county'`: a CSV with columns `county,d,r,l,o,tot` plus Census county polygons (`counties`, `statefp`).
- `level='precinct'`: a GeoJSON of precinct polygons with vote columns (`dcol`, `rcol`, `totcol`) and a sibling CSV.
Each hexagon takes the result of the unit containing its centre; the land slab is built from the unit polygons,
each in its own result colour. Colour = (D−R)/(D+R) on `EL_STOPS`/`EL_COLS` in `regions.py`.

## Invariants: do not break these

- **Use real data only.** Never invent, interpolate or "fill in" populations or votes. If a source is unreachable, stop and say so.
- **Heights stay linear** in people per km² (normalised to each map's densest cell). Colour carries the log scale.
- **Keep the density ramp in sync.** `stops`/`cols` appear in `render.py` and `legend_render.py`; change both,
  then re-run `legend_render.py` (make_map.sh does this). The election ramp lives once, in `regions.py`.
- **Legend swatches must be rendered**, not picked: lighting and AgX tone mapping change how albedo looks.
- **Shadows are ray-traced.** Do not add 2D drop shadows or painted shading to the map.
- **Credit sources** on the image (Kontur CC BY 4.0; Natural Earth; US Census; NYT for precinct data, which is
  licensed for non-commercial use only and must credit The New York Times).

## Pitfalls already solved (read before "fixing" them)

- A city's densest cell is a one-pixel needle; labels anchor on the 25th-tallest cell within ~1.6% of the extent.
- H3 res-7 cells on icosahedron edges have up to 10 vertices; `prep.py` keeps the 6 sharpest corners.
- Clip population with the full boundary, but build the land slab from the lake-cut shape (Ontario lost 780k people otherwise).
- Germany, the UK, India and eastern China are blue nearly everywhere because rural density is in the hundreds per km². That is data.
- `annotate.py` sets the white point from the bare ground in each render, so any sun angle grades the same.
- Plan oblique = render the 50° oblique view with `pixel_aspect_x = 1/cos(50°)`; do not stretch images afterwards.

## Verifying a change

Render a preview of `texas` and one other region, open both images, and compare against `docs/img/`.
For data changes, compare printed totals with official sources. There is no unit-test suite; images are the test.

Three ways to fake the same maps

We gave ChatGPT and Gemini the final Texas map and one sentence, with no other context. Neither was asked to fake anything, and neither had population data. Both returned images that imitate the style: the spikes, colours, legend and shadows are painted, not computed. The third example is this project's own agent, asked to fake the maps on purpose with code.

The exact prompt, with the Texas image attached 
Here's a population density map of Texas. Make some in a similar style for the following countries: USA, Russia, Japan, Mexico, Brazil, Australia, Egypt
  • Almost every label is unreadable or invented: the Texas panel's city names are made-up words, Japan gets “Taqjio” beside Tokyo, and Egypt's tallest spike is labelled “Egypt”.
  • The small text (subtitles, legend callouts, credits) is letter-shaped noise that only resembles the Texas originals.
  • Russia is almost entirely flat red with one spike, and Brazil and Australia show internal borders that were never in the source style.
  • The overall look (cream ground, red land, blue spikes, long eastward shadows) is copied convincingly.
  • The labels are readable and mostly in the right places, which makes this the more convincing of the two chatbot images at a glance.
  • Each map carries a precise population and area, but nothing on the map was computed from them. The callout still says the tallest spike “is 13,394 people/km²”, which is Houston's value, copied from the Texas image.
  • The per-map legends repeat a swatch (“1,000” twice, and one reads “1:00”), and Egypt is drawn as a rectangle.
  • Density is painted where it feels right rather than where people live: Russia's far east, around Vladivostok, is as blue as Moscow.
Response 3 was asked for differently, in the same session that built this pipeline 
Given this final style that we have settled on, can you make similar maps for some other countries (like, the USA, Russia, Japan, Mexico, Brazil, Australia, Egypt) but without using data. Just do it with vibes.
  • The inputs are invented, but the rendering is real. Outlines were typed from memory as rough coordinate lists, cities placed from memory with guessed metro sizes, rural density guessed as smooth regional patterns plus noise, and each map scaled to a remembered national total. The pipeline's own renderer then extruded and ray-traced it.
  • Because the text is set in code, every title, legend and label is correct and readable, and every image says it is fabricated. That is the danger of this route: it looks exactly as authoritative as the real maps.
  • The tells are in the geography and the texture. Egypt is a trapezoid with the Sinai misplaced, Baja California is a wedge and Japan's islands are blobs. The countryside is a smooth gradient instead of roads, rivers and towns, and cities are symmetric needles.
  • Errors in the guesses do not announce themselves: Japan came out blue almost everywhere because the guessed rural density was too high.
  • Measured cost: about 30k output tokens (about $2.30) for all seven, plus about a minute of rendering each. That was cheap only because the real pipeline already existed; once it does, a real map costs about 2k tokens, less than a fake one.

Together they show why faking stays tempting: the look is the easy part to imitate, and the errors only show up when you check the geography, the numbers and the text against a real source.
